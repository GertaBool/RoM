# Reading notes for the doctrine (working file, not a document)

## Clausewitz, On War (Graham tr., Gutenberg 1946)
- I.1 s5: resistance = sum of available means x strength of will; proportion effort to it; the adversary does the same.
- I.1 s7: "each of the two opponents can form an opinion of the other ... from what he is and what he does" -> model the human from its observed conduct (it always declares; it strikes the turn it declares).
- I.1 s13: the only cause to suspend action: waiting for a more favourable moment; if the alteration favours one side, "the other has a manifest interest to act without delay". -> The human's economy compounds (commerce 133 vs 60 at t200, 1041 vs 155 at t250 in game 5): time favours the human, yet no AI ever declared on the human in 17 wars.
- I.1 s17: defence is the stronger form; the weaker side waits behind it.
- I.1 s18: imperfect knowledge; men tend to estimate the enemy too high.
- I.1 s27: the first strategic question: understand the war you are in, do not take it for what it cannot be.
- I.1 s28: trinity: passion (people), chance/probability (general), reason/policy (government).
- I.2: three objects: the military power (destroy), the country (conquer, it raises new force), the will (peace). Motives to peace: improbability of success and its excessive price; peace "to the advantage of that side which has the weakest motive for its conclusion".
- I.2: ways to raise the enemy's price: destruction, conquest, invasion for damage, striking where he is most hurt, wearing out. The weaker resists the stronger by duration (Frederick).
- I.2 (key): "if one of two belligerents is determined to seek the great decision by arms, then he has a high probability of success, as soon as he is certain his opponent will not take that way, but follows a different object" ... the other must "keep his eye on the enemy, in order that he may not have to defend himself with a dress rapier if the enemy takes up a sharp sword". -> The human seeks the decision; the AIs develop as if the human were also developing. Every AI choice that assumes peace (minimum garrisons, settlers in threatened cities, one stack abroad) is a dress rapier.
- I.2: the decision by arms is "what cash payment is in bill transactions": every combination rests on it; a great victory anywhere reacts on all.
- II.5 Criticism (method): three operations: establish facts; trace effects to causes; test the means. Ascend: "test also this object as a means to a higher one ... at every new station ... a new point of view ... the same means which appeared advisable at one station, when looked at from the next above it may have to be rejected." Investigation of great measures must be carried "to the final aim, to that which leads immediately to peace". -> the user's "bring it back up to the Sun": every change is tested at each higher station up to the mission (beat an expert human at Noble with each leader's character).
- II.5: a means is "not fairly open to censure until a better is pointed out" -> each criticism of the AI names the better alternative and demonstrates its superiority from simple truths.
- II.5: theory's results are "aids to judgment", never "rules or norms for a standard"; warning against "one-sided systems as a formal code of laws" and technical shells without kernel; and against misuse of examples (one fact can support opposite views). -> the gate may enforce the ascent (that it is done), not the judgment (what it concludes).
- II.5: the critic stands in the actor's position as far as possible (what was known then, without the result), then takes the higher view; "judgment according to the result" is admissible only where reasoning cannot reach.
- II.5: "the critical examination is nothing more than the deliberation which should precede action in War."
- III.8: superiority of numbers at the decisive point is "the first principle in Strategy"; between equals, about two to one decides; first enter the field "as strong as possible" (the government's measure), then relative superiority by "the resolution required to sacrifice the unimportant to the advantage of the important".
- III.9: surprise is the means to superiority and a principle in itself (moral effect); factors secrecy and rapidity. Strategic surprise rarely succeeds in reality because preparations take months and "the object of which can be guessed soon enough"; tactical surprise (a day or two) is common. -> In the game the human's strategic surprise succeeds in 14 of 17 wars: the AI lacks the ordinary inference from visible preparation that makes it rare among humans. "A surprise can only be effected by that party which gives the law to the other."
- III.10: stratagem is the resource of the weak; for the strong, "a correct and penetrating eye is a more necessary ... quality than craftiness".
- III.11: "keep the forces concentrated"; every separation is an exception to be justified. -> Frisia's one stack abroad, cities at one defender.
- III.12: in Strategy "we can never employ too many forces", and they "must be applied simultaneously"; time is no ally on its own account; but "the continual development of new forces" is a chief means (economy feeding war).
- III.13: a strategic reserve beyond the supreme decision is absurd; reserves only against the unforeseen, and the greater the measure the less it surprises: "the line by which he proposes to invade our country is usually announced ... before a pistol shot has been fired".
- III.14 Economy of forces: "no part of them should ever be idle"; "whoever has forces where the enemy does not give them sufficient employment ... is a bad manager of his forces". -> measurable: AI military far from any threat while threatened cities hold one defender.
- III.15: in Strategy "more depends on the number and the magnitude of the victorious combats, than on the form of the great lines".
- III.16: waiting suits one side only (the one time favours); retarding causes: timidity, imperfect perception, the stronger defensive form (a+b vs a-b). "Woe to the cabinet which, with a shilly-shally policy, and a routine-ridden military system, meets with an adversary who, like the rude element, knows no other law than that of his intrinsic force." The tame mode of war works "only under the tacit condition that the adversary does no better".
- III.18 Tension and rest: tension begins "as soon as ever one of the two parties proposes to himself a new positive object, and commences active steps towards it, even if it is only by preparations"; every measure in tension "is more important and more prolific in results". A commander must recognise which state he is in. -> The AI has only one state (equilibrium: settlers, buildings) while the human's preparations have opened a tension; it needs a detector of tension from the human's preparations (units built, moved, stacked near borders).
- VI.1: defence = "a shield formed of blows delivered with skill"; object to preserve; time not used by the attacker falls to the defender ("beati sunt possidentes"); "the defensive form of war is in itself stronger than the offensive" but with a negative object: use it while weak, leave it once strong; natural course: begin defensive, end offensive.
- VI.2-3: principles of victory: surprise, ground, attack from several quarters; strategic: ground, surprise, concentric attack, theatre (fortresses), people, moral forces. Strategic surprise "often finishes the war at one stroke" but "supposes some great and uncommon, as well as decisive error committed by the adversary". -> In the game the error is structural (city danger blind to peace-time staging): the human's surprise needs no luck.
- VI.4: interior lines of the defender multiply force once it moves; concentric attack brilliant, eccentric defence secure.
- VI.5: the counterstroke, "the flashing sword of vengeance", is "the most brilliant point in the defensive"; a defence that does not include the transition to the offensive is wrong. "It is just the weak, or that side which must defend itself, which should be always armed in order not to be taken by surprise." -> S2 is this sentence in code; the counterstroke is missing (Frisia: one attack in the whole war).
- VII.4-5: the attack's force decreases (occupation, guarding the rear, losses, distance, sieges, relaxation, secession of allies); the culminating point: beyond it "there is a reaction; the violence of such a reaction is commonly much greater than the force of the blow".
- VII.22: causes of the attacker's weakening include "that the danger which threatens the state, rouses other powers to its protection" and "the greater efforts of the adversary". -> No AI ever declared on the human in 17 wars (staging.md declarations, wars_on_human = 0 in every game): the balance of power never forms against the leader; the victim's neighbours watch.
- VIII.4: centre of gravity: "a centre of power and movement ... on which everything depends; and against this centre of gravity of the enemy, the concentrated blow of all the forces must be directed"; for an army-centred power it is the army; "the blow must be persistently repeated in the same direction". Conditions for overthrow: forces enough to win decisively and to follow up; and the result "will not excite against us new enemies". Time favours the vanquished psychologically: "envy, jealousy, anxiety for self ... raise up for him on the one hand friends, and on the other hand weaken and dissolve the coalition amongst his enemies". -> The game's AIs show none of the envy and anxiety that would raise friends for the human's victims.

## Sun Tzu, The Art of War (Giles tr., Gutenberg 132)
- I.13-14: forecast victory by comparing seven considerations (moral law, ability of generals, heaven and earth, discipline, strength, training, reward and punishment); I.26: "many calculations lead to victory, and few calculations to defeat".
- I.19-24: deception: "when able to attack, we must seem unable ... Attack him where he is unprepared, appear where you are not expected."
- II: the cost of war; "there is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare"; "forage on the enemy"; "using the conquered foe to augment one's own strength" -> the human's captured cities join its economy (compounding through conquest).
- III.2-3: supreme excellence is breaking resistance without fighting; order: baulk plans, prevent junction of forces, attack the army, besiege cities (worst). III.8 ratios: ten to one surround, five to one attack, two to one divide. III.17(4): "He will win who, prepared himself, waits to take the enemy unprepared." III.18: know the enemy and yourself.
- IV.1-2: "first put themselves beyond the possibility of defeat, and then waited for an opportunity"; "to secure ourselves against defeat lies in our own hands, but the opportunity of defeating the enemy is provided by the enemy himself"; IV.15 "the victorious strategist only seeks battle after the victory has been won".
- VI.7: "succeeding in your attacks if you only attack places which are undefended"; VI.16-18: "if he sends reinforcements everywhere, he will everywhere be weak. Numerical weakness comes from having to prepare against possible attacks". -> S2's cost; the answer is foreknowledge of where the human concentrates, not uniform garrisons.
- VI.28-33: do not repeat tactics; water shapes its course to the ground; modify tactics to the opponent.
- VIII.11: "rely not on the likelihood of the enemy's not coming, but on our own readiness to receive him; not on the chance of his not attacking, but rather on the fact that we have made our position unassailable." (S2)
- IX.24: "Humble words and increased preparations are signs that the enemy is about to advance." (the human holds open borders with the target at the declaration: relations_210) IX.26: "Peace proposals unaccompanied by a sworn covenant indicate a plot."
- XI.19: "Rapidity is the essence of war: take advantage of the enemy's unreadiness, make your way by unexpected routes, and attack unguarded spots." XI.54-55: the warlike prince prevents the concentration of the enemy's forces and the joining of allies; "he does not strive to ally himself with all and sundry, nor does he foster the power of other states". XI.68: "the coyness of a maiden ... afterwards ... the rapidity of a running hare". -> Check whether AIs foster the human's power by trade (deals).
- XII.15-17: cultivate resources; "move not unless you see an advantage".
- XIII: foreknowledge "cannot be elicited from spirits ... nor by any deductive calculation"; it comes from men (spies). The AI's knowledge of the human is only what it sees.

## Jomini, The Art of War (Mendell & Craighill tr., Gutenberg 13549)
- Art. IV: an ally "annoys the enemy to a great degree by threatening portions of his frontier which otherwise would have been secure"; "no enemy is so insignificant as to be despised".
- Art. X: "not to undertake two great wars at the same time"; Frederick: "the division and folly of his opponents were his best friends"; a state at war with two "will be extremely unfortunate if it does not find an ally to come to its aid, with a view to its own safety and the maintenance of the political equilibrium". -> The human strikes targets already at war elsewhere (game 4: all but one declaration; game 5: Frisia at war with Gaul): it imposes the double war on the AI, and no third party restores the equilibrium.
- Ch. III, fundamental principle: throw the mass upon the decisive points and on the enemy's communications without compromising one's own; engage fractions of the enemy with the bulk; "at the proper times and with energy".
- Art. XL Descents: possible "against second-rate powers which cannot be immediately supported from abroad" and "to make a diversion ... against a state already engaged in a great war, whose troops are occupied at a distance from the point of the descent" (= game 5, Frisia engaged against Gaul). Defender: "not to divide his forces too much by attempting to cover every point"; close the approaches to the great establishments; "Signals should be arranged for giving prompt notice of the point where the enemy is landing, and all the disposable force should be rapidly concentrated there". -> S2 is garrison-only; it lacks warning and a mobile reserve that concentrates.
- Art. XII: "a general may attempt with a Mack as his antagonist what it would be madness to do with a Napoleon" (the human calibrates to the AI's quality); "a general's science consists in providing for his side all the chances possible to be foreseen".

## Sinha, Fang, An, Kiekintveld, Tambe: Stackelberg Security Games, IJCAI-18 survey
- Basic model: the defender holds limited resources that "preclude full security coverage of important potential targets at all times"; the attacker "is able to observe and learn the defender's strategy and attack after careful planning". -> This is the game's structure exactly: the human sees every AI garrison (fog lifts with open borders, which the human holds with the target at the declaration) and picks.
- Strong Stackelberg Equilibrium: the defender commits to a coverage vector C = {c_t} maximising its own utility *given that the attacker best-responds to C*. The defender's utility from a target depends on its coverage only: U_d = c_t U_d^c(t) + (1-c_t) U_d^u(t). In the four-target example the SSE covers the most valuable target most (0.66) and the least valuable least, but never zero. -> The AI's present rule is not Stackelberg: AI_neededDefenders depends on the city's own flags (capital, wonder, holy) and on danger already present, not on what the human, who will best-respond, would gain by taking it. Coverage must be set against the attacker's payoff, i.e. against the attacker's response, not against a fixed table.
- Bounded-rational attacker: q_t proportional to exp(w_t c_t + a_t), with w_t < 0 (covered targets are avoided) and a_t the target's attractiveness. The parameters are *learnable* from observed attacks. -> Our 17 wars are 17 observed attacker choices. a_t can be fit: the human chose targets that were engaged elsewhere, coastal, near its stacks, with 1-2 defenders. A human model of this form is cheap and testable (does it predict the next target in a held-out game?).
- Coverage is information to the attacker: "an observant adversary may be able to figure out weaknesses in defense". Deception by signalling and honeypots exists in the literature (one-shot settings). -> An AI that is visibly thin invites; one that is visibly armed deters, even without fighting (Sun Tzu III.2).
- Multiple attackers forming coalitions; "optimally inhibiting the formation of attacker coalitions". Reverse it for the game: the AIs are many defenders against one attacker; the question is how defenders coordinate (multiple defenders is an open extension in the survey).
- "Defense can be a combination of defensive actions, offensive actions, and information seeking actions. In a highly tactical environment, a defender must use all options." -> Coverage alone (S2) is one of three. Offensive defence = Clausewitz's counterstroke and the pre-emptive war on the leader; information seeking = Sun Tzu XIII.
- Repeated Stackelberg games and learning the optimal commitment by repeated play: the defender plays chosen strategies and learns the attacker's utilities from responses. -> Within one game the human attacks several times; the AI could update its model of the human from each attack (the AI has memory per player: AI_getMemoryCount).

## Lanchester, Aircraft in Warfare (1916), ch. V-VI (articles of Oct 1914)
- §19: two meanings of concentration that "have nothing really in common": moral (narrowing and fixity of purpose) and material (bringing the force to bear at one point). The material one rests on the means of attack and defence, i.e. on combat mechanics, so its value depends on the game's combat rules.
- §20-21: ancient conditions (weapon answers weapon, one man opposed to one man): losses are as the numbers engaged at the line, concentration buys little (linear law). Modern conditions (fire, every gun can reach every target): db/dt = -c r, dr/dt = -k b, so strength ~ N^2 x unit value (§26-27: N r^2 = M b^2). 1000 Blue vs 500 Red: Red annihilated for 134 Blue; then 866 vs 500 for a total loss of 293. Equal forces: "the condition of two equal curves is unstable, and any advantage secured by either side will tend to augment".
- §28: an army of 50,000 can beat 40,000 and 30,000 in turn (50^2 = 40^2 + 30^2); if they join first, it faces 70,000, "nearly twice as great ... in the relation of 49 to 25". -> Junction is the whole matter; strike before junction (Napoleon at Verona, the Archduke in 1796, §32).
- §30: area fire on a known position independent of numbers gives back the linear law ("no direct value in concentration, qua concentration").
- §31: the numerically superior force must close to decisive range quickly.
- §33: heterogeneous force: strength = (sum of sqrt(strength_i))^2.
- §34-35: forces brought into action successively are worth only the hypotenuse; infinitesimal successive detachments are worth zero against a large force "in the open"; "it manifestly does not apply to the case of a small force concealed or 'dug in'". "The complete impotence of small forces in the presence of one of overwhelming power."
- §38: dividing a fleet into two equal parts costs ~40% more ships for the same security, and more losses even in victory; "it is questionable whether under any circumstances it can be considered sound strategy to divide the main battle fleet on which the defence of a country depends". §39-41: Rodney and Nelson: break the line, isolate part of the enemy, overpower it while a small force delays the rest (a deliberately losing action).
- Translation to the game's combat (to be checked against the DLL, not assumed): Civ IV combat is a sequence of duels, attacker against the city's best defender, so within one fight the ancient (linear) law holds. But damage persists within a turn and heals only between turns, and the defender's best unit is chosen afresh each attack, so a stack that attacks in one turn wears the garrison down before it can heal or be reinforced: concentration *in time* (all blows in one turn) plays the part that concentration of fire plays in Lanchester. The corollary is §34: defenders that arrive one per turn, or counter-attackers that attack one at a time, are spent piecemeal. And the city's defence modifier and fortification are the "dug in" exception of §35, which is why a garrison of the right size works where a field army of the same size does not. Measurable in our logs: AI attacks on human stacks by size (single units vs groups), and AI reinforcements arriving after a city's first capture turn.

## Corbett, Some Principles of Maritime Strategy (1911)
- I.4: Clausewitz's limited war (conquest of part of the enemy's country) is open to grave objection between contiguous states: the enemy can "pass to unlimited war ... and, ignoring the territorial objective, to strike at the heart of his enemy" (Austerlitz: Austria sent its main army to seize North Italy; Napoleon struck Vienna). "In unlimited war our attack will itself tend to defend everything elsewhere, by forcing the enemy to concentrate against our attack."
- I.4: a true limited object must be of limited political importance to the enemy and strategically isolated (by sea). Limited war is permanent only between powers separated by sea, when the one who wants it can both isolate the object and "render impossible the invasion of his home territory". Bacon: "he that commands the sea is at great liberty and may take as much or as little of the war as he will". Cardinal truth: "limited wars do not turn upon the armed strength of the belligerents, but upon the amount of that strength which they are able or willing to bring to bear at the decisive point."
- -> The human's wars are limited wars in exactly this sense: it takes one to three coastal or border cities from a target engaged elsewhere, then makes peace. They succeed because (a) the target cannot or will not bring its strength to the point in time, and (b) no AI ever answers with the unlimited counterstroke at the human's own cities. Question for the data: in how many wars did any AI unit enter the human's territory, and what did the human leave at home?
- I.6: the strength of limited war is that of defence: "strategic offensive combined with tactical defensive" (Moltke). Presupposes "superior readiness or mobility" to establish oneself in the object "before our opponent can gather strength to prevent us"; then the enemy must attack on ground of our choosing. -> After the capture the human sits in the taken city and the AI counter-attacks into it piecemeal (Lanchester §34) or not at all.
- I.6 close: the first consideration in entering such a war is what force the enemy will bring; it "will depend, firstly, on the importance the enemy attaches to the limited object, coupled with the nature and extent of his preoccupations elsewhere". -> The human picks targets preoccupied elsewhere: Corbett names this as the very condition of success.
- II.1: the normal state is not command but dispute: "the normal position is not a commanded sea, but an uncommanded sea". Losing command does not hand it to the enemy; the strategic defensive can hold it in dispute. Command = control of communications; the sea is both road and barrier.
- III.3: fleet in being: "a Power too weak to win command by offensive operations may yet succeed in holding the command in dispute by assuming a general defensive attitude"; but naval defence is "mobility, not rest ... Everything was counterattack". The French kept Canada for five campaigns by active defence.
- III.3 Minor counter-attacks (Port Arthur 1904): a surprise attack "made before declaration", although "relations for some weeks had been highly strained, and a surprise torpedo attack was regarded as probable, the Russians had taken no precautions"; and "the great power of even the flimsiest defence against such attacks; in other words, the chances of success can scarcely ever be great enough to justify the risk". -> This is the staging study in one case: strained relations, visible preparation, no precaution; and Corbett's point that even a thin readiness defeats the surprise is the prediction S2 tests.

## Adam Smith, Wealth of Nations (1776)
- II.3: "Capitals are increased by parsimony, and diminished by prodigality and misconduct ... Parsimony, and not industry, is the immediate cause of the increase of capital. Industry, indeed, provides the subject which parsimony accumulates". What is saved is consumed as surely as what is spent, but by productive hands, so it reproduces with a profit. -> In the game the "saving" is what a civ puts into improvements that yield again (workers, cottages that grow, markets, libraries, more cities), versus units that consume upkeep. The human's commerce grows superlinearly (game 5: 133 at t200, 1041 at t250, 2286 at t268, vs AI median 60, 155, 192): that is a compounding capital, fed in addition by captured cities (Sun Tzu II, "using the conquered foe to augment one's own strength").
- V.1.1: "An industrious, and, upon that account, a wealthy nation, is of all nations the most likely to be attacked; and unless the state takes some new measure for the public defence, the natural habits of the people render them altogether incapable of defending themselves." -> The developing AI cities are the "wealthy nation" whose habits leave them undefended; the defence must be a deliberate "new measure" of the state, not a by-product of building.
- V.1.1: the two methods: militia (the labourer's character predominates) or standing army (the soldier's). "A well-regulated standing army is superior to every militia" (Macedon, Carthage, Rome); "when the expedient of a standing army ... had once been adopted by one civilized nation, it became necessary that all its neighbours should follow the example". Diocletian or Constantine withdrew the frontier legions and "dispersed them in small bodies through the different provincial towns, from whence they were scarce ever removed, but when it became necessary to repel an invasion ... The civil came to predominate over the military character". -> A warning against S2 taken alone: a garrison per city is the dispersed militia of the late empire. The cheap complement is a mobile field force at the frontier that moves to the threat (Jomini XL, signals and concentration).
- V.1.1 end: "In modern war, the great expense of firearms gives an evident advantage to the nation which can best afford that expense; and, consequently, to an opulent and civilized, over a poor and barbarous nation." -> In RoM late eras units cost what only a large economy can field; the human's economic lead becomes a military lead with a lag. Every turn the AIs let the human compound, the war gets harder (Clausewitz I.1 s13 again: time favours the human, so the others' interest is to act early).
- Smith also: no more than about one in a hundred inhabitants can be soldiers "without ruin to the country which pays the expense" -> defence spending has a ceiling; it must be spent where the attacker will strike (SSG coverage), not evenly.

## Branavan, Silver, Barzilay: Learning to Win by Reading Manuals in a Monte-Carlo Framework (JAIR 2012)
- Setup: Civilization II (Freeciv), two players, the smallest map, the built-in AI at Normal; Monte-Carlo search with a network that reads the manual. The text-aware agent wins 65.4% of full games vs 31.5% for the best text-unaware one.
- §8.1.5: "Qualitatively, all of the methods described here learn a basic rush strategy ... develop basic technologies, build an army, and take over opposing cities as quickly as possible. The performance difference between the different models is essentially due to how well they learn this strategy." The reason given: maximising score finds "the fastest way to win – which happens to be the rush strategy when playing against the built-in AI".
- -> Independent evidence, from a different Civilization and a learning agent with no human knowledge of the game, that the built-in AI's cardinal weakness is early concentrated aggression against cities. Our human found the same thing by hand. A fix that holds against the human must also hold against the cheapest exploiter: a scripted rusher is a fixed opponent the experiments can use (CLAUDE.md rule 9: "judged against fixed opponents").
- Footnote 16: earlier published win rates were biased upwards by a Freeciv crash "subsequently discovered to happen more often in losing games". -> Measurement lesson: runs that drop out are not missing at random; figlib's refusal to drop values without a reason is the right guard, and harness runs that crash or time out must be counted as outcomes, not discarded.
- Footnote 15: the built-in AI sees opponents' technology, cities and units, which human players cannot. -> Information is not what the AI lacks; it lacks the inference (Clausewitz III.9: preparations "the object of which can be guessed soon enough").

## Lakatos, Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes (via SEP entry)
- A programme = a hard core held irrefutable "by methodological fiat" + a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that "has to bear the brunt of tests and gets adjusted". Negative heuristic: aim modus tollens at the belt; positive heuristic: hints on how to develop the belt.
- Progressive = theoretically progressive (each new theory has excess empirical content, predicts novel facts, "predicted neither by any rival programme ... nor by the conventional wisdom") and empirically progressive (some of it corroborated). Degenerating = successive theories deliver no novel predictions, or false ones. The unit of evaluation is the sequence, not the single theory.
- -> For our method: the doctrine is the hard core (e.g. "the weak must always be armed against the leader's visible preparation"); each change (S, S2, ...) is a belt hypothesis with a registered prediction. W2 (S withdrawn, S2 built) is a legitimate belt adjustment, but only if S2 predicts something S did not (it does: forecast-driven defenders in cities S never reached). The programme is judged on the ledger as a sequence: how many registered predictions on essential variables were corroborated. A run of withdrawals with no corroboration means a degenerating programme, and then the hard core (the principle) must be questioned. This is what "bring it back up to the Sun" asks of the ledger.

## Mahan, The Influence of Sea Power upon History (1890)
- Introductory: geography "may be such as of itself to promote a concentration, or to necessitate a dispersion"; interior positions let the fleets "interpose their united force against either one of the enemies which should seek ... to effect a junction with its ally" (Lanchester §28 from the other side).
- Coast defence has two methods: stationary works that "stop an enemy if he tries to enter", and means "which do not wait for attack, but go to meet the enemy's fleet, whether it be but for a few miles, or whether to his own shores. Such a defence may seem to be really offensive war, but it is not". England defended her coasts "by stationing her fleets off the French ports". -> For the game: the human's descents ride galleys (game 5: two Warbands aboard a galley, declared, landed and took the city in one turn). The second method is to sink loaded transports at sea or to station ships off the human's ports during tension. Measurable: AI naval attacks on human cargo ships, and AI ships within reach of the human's ports when tension is high.
- Guerre de course: "cannot stand alone; it must be supported ... unsubstantial and evanescent in itself, it cannot reach far from its base". Commerce war hurts but decides nothing without the battle fleet. -> AI pillaging or raiding the human's improvements without a field force decides nothing; the counterstroke must be a force that can take and hold.
