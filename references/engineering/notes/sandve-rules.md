## What it says
- p. 1 (Introduction): "Replication is the cornerstone of a cumulative science" -- but new tools, massive data, interdisciplinary approaches and pressure on scientists complicate it.
- p. 1 (Introduction): "there has recently been a call for reproducible research as an attainable minimum standard for assessing the value of scientific claims" -- since full replication on independently collected data is often not feasible.
- p. 1 (Introduction): this requires papers to "provide a sufficiently clear protocol to allow successful repetition and extension of analyses based on original data"
- p. 1 (Introduction): the evidence cited: papers "commonly leave out experimental details essential for reproduction" -- plus difficulties replicating published results, more retracted papers and many failing clinical trials.
- p. 1 (Introduction): "a lack of reproducibility can also be a burden for you as an individual researcher." -- good habits "may actually turn out to be a time-saver in the longer run."
- p. 1 (Introduction): "reproducibility is just as much about the habits that ensure reproducible research as the technologies that can make these processes efficient and realistic."
- p. 1 (Introduction): with a bare-bones approach of custom scripts "you will probably need to handle each rule explicitly" -- integrated frameworks (GenePattern, Galaxy, LONI, Taverna) may already support most rules.
- p. 1 (Introduction): "one may face the need to make a trade-off between the ideals of reproducibility and the need to get the research out while it is still relevant." -- especially since many analyses tried never yield results.
- p. 1 (Introduction): "it may already be too late to take the necessary notes from memory" -- with hindsight one regrets the missed opportunity; the authors believe the rewards compensate for time spent on blind alleys.
- p. 1 (Introduction): "As a minimal requirement, you should at least be able to reproduce the results yourself." -- so that future questioning can be met with a precise explanation.
- p. 1 (Introduction): "There will for a given analysis be an exponential number of possible combinations of software versions, parameter values, preprocessing steps" -- so failing to take notes "may make exact reproduction essentially impossible."
- p. 1 (Introduction): further levels: routinely reusing previous work, and letting peers reproduce results, which "can lead to increased trust in, interest for, and citations of your work"
- p. 2 (Introduction): the rules are for making research more accessible "be it for peers or for your future self."
- p. 2 (Rule 1): "Whenever a result may be of potential interest, keep track of how it was produced." -- the sequence of steps from raw data to result is the analysis workflow, and pre- and post-processing steps are often critical.
- p. 2 (Rule 1): "If the step is performed by a computer program, the critical details include the name and version of the program, as well as the exact parameters and inputs that were used."
- p. 2 (Rule 1): "the documentation can easily get out of sync with how the analysis was really performed in its final version." -- an executable specification (shell scripts, makefiles, stored workflows) ensures it matches what was performed.
- p. 2 (Rule 1): minimum: "record sufficient details on programs, parameters, and manual procedures to allow yourself, in a year or so, to approximately reproduce the results."
- p. 2 (Rule 2): "Whenever possible, rely on the execution of programs instead of manual procedures to modify data." -- "Such manual procedures are not only inefficient and error-prone, they are also difficult to reproduce."
- p. 2 (Rule 2): "manual tweaking of data files to attain format compatibility should be replaced by format converters that can be reenacted" -- copy and paste between documents should also be avoided.
- p. 2 (Rule 2): minimum: "note down which data files were modified or moved, and for what purpose."
- p. 2 (Rule 3): "a newer version of a program may not even run without modifying its inputs." -- and older versions can be hard to obtain, so archive the exact versions actually used.
- p. 2 (Rule 3): "the only viable solution may then be to store a full virtual machine image of the operating system and program." -- when programs depend on other packages or OS components; minimum: "note the exact names and versions of the main programs you use."
- p. 2 (Rule 4): "Even the slightest change to a computer program can have large intended or unintended consequences." -- only the exact state of a script may produce an exact output.
- p. 2 (Rule 4): "backtracking to a code state that gave a certain result may be a ... hopeless task. This can cast doubt on previous results" -- since one cannot know if they came from a bug.
- p. 2 (Rule 4): "The standard solution to track evolution of code is to use a version control system" -- minimum: archive copies of scripts from time to time.
- p. 2 (Rule 5): "In principle, as long as the full process used to produce a given result is tracked, all intermediate data can also be regenerated." -- but accessible intermediate results are of great value in practice.
- p. 2 (Rule 5): "Quickly browsing through intermediate results can reveal discrepancies toward what is assumed" -- uncovering "bugs or faulty interpretations that are not apparent in the final results."
- p. 2 (Rule 5): further reasons: reveals consequences of alternative choices at single steps, allows partial reruns, "allows any experienced inconsistencies to be tracked to the steps where the problems arise", and allows examination without operational executables.
- p. 2 (Rule 5): minimum: "archive any intermediate result files that are produced when running an analysis" -- in standardized formats where possible, if storage is not prohibitive.
- p. 2 (Rule 6): "given the same initial seed, all random numbers used in an analysis will be equal, thus giving identical results every time it is run."
- p. 3 (Rule 6): "While achieving equal results is a strong indication that a procedure has been reproduced exactly, it is often hard to conclude anything when achieving only approximately equal results." -- so record the seed.
- p. 3 (Rule 6): minimum: "note which analysis steps involve randomness, so that a certain level of discrepancy can be anticipated"
- p. 3 (Rule 7): "If raw data behind figures are stored in a systematic manner" -- one can modify the plotting instead of redoing the analysis, and read fine values from the raw numbers.
- p. 3 (Rule 7): "it can be useful to store both the underlying data and the processed values that are directly visualized." -- e.g. for a histogram both the values before binning and the counts per bin; also store the plotting code.
- p. 3 (Rule 7): minimum: "one should note which data formed the basis of a given plot and how this data could be reconstructed."
- p. 3 (Rule 8): "The final results that make it to an article, be it plots or tables, often represent highly summarized data."
- p. 3 (Rule 8): "A common but impractical way of doing this is to incorporate various debug outputs in the source code" -- better to output all underlying data permanently with a systematic naming convention, e.g. hypertext with links from each summary to its data.
- p. 3 (Rule 8): minimum: "at least once generate, inspect, and validate the detailed values underlying the summaries."
- p. 3 (Rule 9): "results usually live on a data area on a server or personal computer, while interpretations live in text documents" -- although interconnected conceptually they "tend to live quite separate lives".
- p. 3 (Rule 9): "Such textual interpretations are not generally mere shadows of the results" -- they carry extra information while needing support in a given result.
- p. 3 (Rule 9): "Making this connection when it is needed may be difficult and error-prone" -- it may be hard to locate the result from a large pool of analyses with various versions.
- p. 3 (Rule 9): "we suggest that statements are connected to underlying results already from the time the statements are initially formulated" -- e.g. a file path or result ID in the text, or tools like Sweave, the GenePattern Word add-in and Galaxy Pages.
- p. 3 (Rule 9): minimum: "provide enough details along with your textual interpretations so as to allow the exact underlying results, or at least some related results, to be tracked down in the future."
- p. 3 (Rule 10): "all input data, scripts, versions, parameters, and intermediate results should be made publicly and easily accessible." -- minimum: submit the main data and source code as supplementary material and answer requests.
- p. 3 (Rule 10): "Making reproducibility of your work by peers a realistic possibility sends a strong signal of quality, trustworthiness, and transparency." -- which could improve review speed, publication chances and citation.

## Readings for our work
- Every figure in an analysis document should carry a pointer to the exact result it rests on (registry entry, query, save or log id, build), written when the sentence is written, not reconstructed later. Our figure system does this for numbers; check that prose claims (readings) also cite the bullet or registry id they rest on. Rests on: Rule 9 connect statements from the start (p. 3), Rule 1 critical details (p. 2). Would not apply if: a statement contains no empirical claim.
- Record the seed of every harness run with its result, and treat a re-run with the same seed and build that does not reproduce exactly as a defect in the harness or build, not as noise. Rests on: Rule 6 seeds and exact versus approximate reproduction (pp. 2-3). Would not apply if: the game engine has non-seeded randomness (e.g. threading or time), in which case note which steps are random and expect discrepancy.
- Archive the exact toolchain used to build the DLL (MSVC 2003, Wine version, build flags) and the hash of each built DLL used in an experiment; a container or VM image may be the only viable way to keep it buildable. Rests on: Rule 3 archive exact versions, full VM image (p. 2). Would not apply if: the DLL binary itself is archived for every experiment and never rebuilt.
- No manual edits to logs, saves, tables or generated documents; every change goes through a script that can be re-run, and any unavoidable manual step is written down with its purpose. Rests on: Rule 2 avoid manual data manipulation (p. 2). Would not apply if: the item is hand-written source text.
- Keep intermediate DuckDB tables or exports per analysis, so a surprising figure can be traced to the step where it diverges, and inspect the per-turn, per-player values behind every summary at least once. Rests on: Rule 5 intermediate results (p. 2), Rule 8 inspect detailed values (p. 3). Would not apply if: storage of a run's intermediates is prohibitive, in which case keep the script and inputs.
- Write claims down from the sources at the time, not from memory afterwards; the authors note it may already be too late to take notes from memory. Rests on: introduction on notes from memory (p. 1). Would not apply if: the claim is re-verified against the source before it is used.
- Store the data behind each plot next to the plot, both the raw values and the binned or summarised values shown. Rests on: Rule 7 (p. 3). Would not apply if: the plot is regenerated from a registry that already holds those values.
