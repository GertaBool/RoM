## What it says
- p. 1 (Abstract): "has led to a proliferation of many thousands or millions of versions of the same datasets in many scientific and commercial domains"
- p. 1 (Abstract): "The fundamental challenge here is the storage-recreation trade-off: the more storage we use, the faster it is to recreate or retrieve versions"
- p. 1 (Abstract): "we formulate six problems under various settings, trading off these quantities in various ways, demonstrate that most of the problems are intractable" -- and propose inexpensive heuristics from delay-constrained scheduling and spanning tree literature.
- p. 1 (1 Introduction): "many datasets are repeatedly analyzed in slightly different ways, with the intermediate results stored for future use." -- the intermediate result scenario.
- p. 1 (1 Introduction): "the datasets being analyzed might be slightly different (e.g., results of simple transformations or cleaning operations, or small updates), but are still stored in their entirety."
- p. 1 (1 Introduction): "often the computation required to recompute a given version from another one is small enough to not merit storing a new version."
- p. 1 (1 Introduction): "they make a private copy, perform modifications via cleansing, normalization, adding new fields or rows, and then store these modified versions back to a folder shared across the entire group." -- a computational biology group; massive redundancy.
- p. 1 (1 Introduction): "it is essential to keep track of versions of datasets and be able to recreate them on demand"
- p. 1 (1 Introduction): DataHub keeps track of datasets and versions "represented in the form of a directed version graph that encodes derivation relationships"
- p. 1 (1 Introduction): "The two goals conflict with each other — minimizing storage cost typically leads to increased recreation costs and vice versa."
- pp. 1-2 (Example 1): storing all five versions in full costs "10000 + 10100 + 9700 + 9800 + 10120 = 49720" in storage; storing one in full and the rest as deltas costs "10000 + 200 + 1000 + 50 + 200 = 11450" but V5 then costs 13550 to recreate instead of 10120.
- p. 2 (1 Introduction): "Version Control Systems (VCS) like Git, SVN, or Mercurial, despite their popularity, use fairly simple algorithms underneath, and are known to have significant limitations when managing large datasets"
- p. 2 (1 Introduction): "Much of the prior work in literature focuses on a linear chain of versions, or on minimizing the storage cost while ignoring the recreation cost"
- p. 2 (1 Introduction): "some mechanisms for constructing differences between versions lead to symmetric differences (either version can be recreated from the other version) — we call this the undirected case."
- p. 2 (1 Introduction): "In some cases the recreation cost is proportional to the storage cost (e.g., if the system bottleneck lies in the I/O cost or network communication), but that may not be true when the system bottleneck is CPU computation."
- p. 2 (1 Introduction): "computing deltas for all pairs of versions is typically not feasible. Relying purely on the version graph may not be sufficient and significant redundancies across datasets may be missed."
- p. 2 (1 Introduction): "In this paper, we focus on the static, off-line version of this problem" -- the online version, with versions added continuously, is left for the future.
- p. 2 (1 Contributions): "We show that most of the variations of this problem are NP-Hard (Section 3)." -- two light-weight heuristics and an adapted prior solution; a prototype and experiments on synthetic and real-world workloads.
- p. 2 (2.1 Version Graph): "A directed edge from Vi to Vj in G(V, E) represents that Vj was derived from Vi" -- with branching and merging the graph is a DAG.
- p. 3 (2.1 Storage and Recreation): "A version that is stored in its entirety is said to be materialized."
- p. 3 (2.1): "We refer to the information needed to construct version Vi from version Vj as the delta from Vj to Vi . The algorithm giving us the delta is called a differencing algorithm."
- p. 3 (2.1): the costs are two matrices; "they capture all the relevant information about the versions for managing and retrieving them."
- p. 3 (2.1 Delta Variants): deltas can be UNIX-style diffs, "a listing of a program, script, SQL query, or command that generates version Vi from Vj" -- or an XOR, or cell-level differences for tabular data; stored compressed or not.
- p. 3 (2.1): "recreation cost is more complicated: it could depend on the network bandwidth (if versions or deltas are stored remotely), the I/O bandwidth, and the computation costs"
- p. 3 (Example 2): "the specific numbers reported here are fictitious and not the result of running any specific algorithm" -- for the revealed matrix entries.
- pp. 3-4 (2.1 Discussion): "Thus, we must reason with incomplete ∆ and Φ matrices." -- deltas restricted to version graph edges or close-by versions; "We assume that some mechanism to choose which deltas to reveal is provided to us."
- p. 4 (2.1 Multiple delta mechanisms): "we can store a program used to derive Vj from Vi , which could take longer to run (i.e., the recreation cost is higher) but is more compact" -- the paper picks one delta mechanism per pair for simplicity.
- p. 4 (2.1 Branches): "we assume each version is either stored in its entirety or stored as a delta from a single other version, even if it is derived from two different datasets."
- p. 4 (2.1 Matrix Properties): "it may be possible to represent the delta from Vi to Vj using a command like: delete all tuples with age > 60, very compactly." -- the reverse delta is likely large.
- p. 4 (2.1): three scenarios: undirected with recreation equal to storage cost, directed with them equal, and directed with them independent; for the proportional case "the problem statements, algorithms and guarantees are unaffected by having a constant proportionality factor."
- p. 4 (2.1 Objective): a valid solution is one "where it is possible to reconstruct any of the original versions." -- every version reachable from a materialized one through a chain of deltas.
- p. 4 (2.1): total storage cost C is the sum over stored materializations and deltas; the recreation cost of a version "is the minimum of these quantities over all sequences that can be used to recreate Vi ."
- p. 4 (2.1 Problem Formulations): Problem 1 minimizes storage; Problem 2 minimizes every recreation cost; Problems 3 and 4 minimize sum or max recreation "under the constraint that total storage cost C is smaller than some threshold β. These problems are relevant when the storage budget is limited."
- pp. 4-5 (2.1): Problems 5 and 6 minimize storage subject to a bound θ on sum or max recreation cost -- "relevant when we want to reduce the storage cost, but must satisfy some constraints on the recreation costs."
- p. 5 (2.2 Mapping to Graph Formulation): "An edge in the version graph indicates a derivation relationship, whereas an edge in G simply indicates that it is possible to recreate the target version using the source version" -- G has a dummy root V0 whose edges stand for materialization and may contain cycles.
- p. 5 (2.2 Lemma 1): "The optimal storage graph Gs = (Vs , Es ) for all 6 problems listed above must be a spanning tree T rooted at dummy vertex V0 in graph G."
- p. 5 (2.2 Lemmas 2-3): the optimum for Problem 1 is a minimum spanning tree on storage weights; for Problem 2 a shortest path tree on recreation weights.
- p. 5 (2.3 ILP Formulation): Problem 6 is written as an integer linear program with a binary variable per edge, using a sufficiently large constant to express the if-then constraint (Lemma 4: equivalence).
- p. 6 (3 Computational Complexity): "Problem 1 and 2 can be solved in polynomial time by directly applying a minimum spanning tree algorithm" -- Kruskal or Prim undirected, Edmonds directed; Dijkstra for Problem 2.
- p. 6 (3 Triangle Inequality): "since, in our case, deltas represent actual modifications that can be stored, it must obey additional realistic constraints." -- this makes hardness proofs harder.
- p. 6 (3 Lemma 5): "Problem 6 is NP-hard when ∆ = Φ and Φ is sym-" metric -- by reduction from set cover; Lemma 6 (p. 7) does the same for Problem 5.
- p. 8 (3): "we can conclude that Problem 3, 4, 5, 6, are NP-hard in a more general setting" -- Problems 3 and 4 follow from 5 and 6.
- p. 8 (3 Hop-Based Variants): with every delta costing one hop, Problem 6 matches the bounded-diameter minimum spanning tree; Lemma 8: it "has no ln n- approximation" under a complexity assumption; "It remains to be seen if hardness of approximation can be demonstrated for the variants in the second and third last columns."
- p. 8 (4 Proposed Algorithms): "LMG, or the Local Move Greedy algorithm, tailored to the case when there is a bound or objective on the average recreation cost" -- Problems 3 and 5.

- pp. 8-9 (4.1 LMG): "the algorithm starts with the Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) as GS , and then greedily adds edges from the Shortest Path Tree (SPT) that are not present in GS , while GS respects the bound on storage cost."

## Readings for our work
- Generated artefacts (rendered documents, registries, tables derived from logs) are deltas whose recreation cost is the time to rerun the generator; if regeneration is cheap and deterministic, commit only the source and the recipe and regenerate, which also removes the temptation to hand-edit the output. Check: a clean checkout plus the generator reproduces the committed outputs byte for byte. Rests on: recompute is often cheaper than storing (p. 1), a delta can be a script that generates the version (p. 3), program deltas are compact but slower (p. 4). Would not apply if: regeneration needs the game, Wine or many hours of harness runs (then store the output, with the recipe and inputs recorded).
- Raw game logs and saves from seeded runs should be stored in full (materialized) because they cannot be recreated cheaply; derived tables may be stored as recipes. The choice per artefact should be stated as storage cost against recreation cost. Rests on: storage-recreation trade-off (p. 1), materialized versions (p. 3). Would not apply if: the harness is fully deterministic and fast enough that rerunning a seed is cheaper than keeping its log.
- Record derivation edges explicitly (which version of which input and which tool produced each artefact), because the version graph of derivation is different from how things happen to be stored and cannot be reconstructed from storage alone. Rests on: version graph encodes derivation (pp. 1-2), storage graph edges only mean recreatable (p. 5). Would not apply if: every artefact is produced by one fixed pipeline from one input.
- Patches to the legacy C++ DLL are deltas in the paper's sense; a delta that also rewrites line endings or whitespace is large and couples unrelated changes, so the diff of a patch should be checked for line-ending changes (CRLF) before it is delivered. Check: the patch diff ignoring whitespace equals the plain diff. Rests on: line-based diffs as the delta mechanism of source control (pp. 3, 15), delta size as storage cost (p. 3). Would not apply if: a line-ending normalization is the deliberate, separate content of its own commit.
- Merges should be explicit and made by a person, recorded as a version with two parents, rather than relied upon to be automatic; for patches to the DLL repository this means resolving conflicts by hand and saying so in the merge request. Rests on: the prototype lets users perform merges (p. 11), one delta per version even when derived from two (p. 4). Would not apply if: the two branches touch disjoint files.
- Reported numbers that are illustrative should be marked as such, as the authors mark their fictitious matrix entries; in our documents an illustrative figure must not enter the figure registry as a measured one. Rests on: Example 2 fictitious numbers (p. 3), ILP results reported as best found, not optimal (p. 14). Would not apply if: no illustrative numbers are used.
- Claims about a tool's internal behaviour (as with git's packing) should be taken from its source at a named commit, not from discussion or memory, as the authors did. Rests on: git's algorithm not clearly described, undocumented changes, source cloned at a commit (p. 16). Would not apply if: the tool has an authoritative specification.
