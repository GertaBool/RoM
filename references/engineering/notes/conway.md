# Melvin E. Conway, How Do Committees Invent? (Datamation, April 1968)
Source: lit3/conway/doc.txt. Read: lines 1-490 (all; the extracted text interleaves columns and figure labels).

## What it says
- p. 1 (opening): "That kind of intellectual activity which creates a useful whole from its diverse parts may be called the design of a system." -- the definition is meant to cover weapon systems, social recommendations and programming alike.
- p. 1 (opening): "the objective of a design organization is the creation and assembly of a document containing a coherently structured body of information." -- this body of information is named the system design, produced for a sponsor.
- p. 1 (opening): "The design organization may or may not be involved in the construction of the system it designs." -- public affairs often discourage a group acting on its own recommendations; private industry often the opposite.
- p. 1 (opening): "they may also be personal decisions the designer makes about his own future." -- design choices are affected by whether the designer will have to carry out the recommendations.
- p. 1 (opening): "the incentives which exist in a conventional management environment can motivate choices which subvert the intent of the sponsor."
- p. 1 (stages of design): "The initial stages .of a design effort are concerned more with structuring of the design activity than with the system" -- the preliminary milestones are understanding the boundaries and a preliminary notion of the system's organization so that task groups can be assigned.
- p. 1 (stages of design): "there is a class of design alternatives which cannot be effectively pursued by such an organization because the necessary communication paths do not exist." -- organizing a design team means some design decisions are already made.
- p. 1 (stages of design): "Therefore, there is no such thing as a design group which is both organized and unbiased."
- p. 1 (stages of design): "Every time a delegation is made and somebody's scope of inquiry is narrowed, the class of design alternatives which can be effectively pursued is also narrowed."
- p. 1 (stages of design): "Coordination among task groups, although it appears to lower the productivity of the individual in the small group, provides the only possibility" -- that separate task groups consolidate their efforts into a unified design.
- p. 1 (stages of design): "Drawing of boundaries according to the ground rules." -- first of five stages: boundaries, preliminary concept, organization and delegation by that concept, coordination, consolidation of subdesigns.
- p. 1 (stages of design): "the very act of voluntarily abandoning a creation is painful and expensive." -- a design activity may reorganize on discovering a superior concept, but this is unflattering and costly.
- p. 2 (stages of design): "there's never enough time to do something right, but there's always enough time to do it over." -- from the historian's view the process is continually repeating.
- p. 2 (the designed system): "Any system of consequence is structured from smaller subsystems which are interconnected." -- a description must give the connections to the outside world and each subsystem and its interconnections, recursively, down to parts simple enough to understand.
- p. 2 (the designed system): "It may be less obvious that a theory is a system in the same sense." -- a theory must explain, or not contradict, observed events and consists of subtheories, as in an air-crash investigation.
- p. 2 (linear graphs): "refers to the inter-subsystem communication path or branch represented by a line in Fig. 1." -- the definition of interface in the linear-graph view.
- p. 2 (relating the two): "an abstraction which has the same form for the two entities we are considering: the design organization and the system it designs." -- replace system by committee, subsystem by subcommittee, interface by coordinator.
- p. 2 (a basic relationship): "the relationship is so simple that in some cases it is an identity." -- the answer to whether there is a predictable relationship between the graph of the organization and the graph of the system.
- p. 2 (the proof): "For any node x in the system we can identify a design group of the design organization which designed x" -- the mapping need not be one-to-one.
- p. 2 (the proof): "must have negotiated and agreed upon an interface specification to permit communication between the two corresponding nodes of the design organization." -- if two subsystems communicate, their design groups communicated; if not, there was nothing to negotiate.
- p. 3 (the proof, footnote): "It may be trivial, hinging on the definition of meaningful negotiation." -- alternatively it rests on the observation that one group almost never compromises its own design for another group unless imperative.
- p. 3 (the proof): "there is a very close relationship between the structure of a system and the structure of the organization which designed it." -- where one group designed several subsystems, the organization is a collapsed version of the system.
- p. 3 (the proof): "This kind of a structure-preserving relationship between two sets of things is called a homomorphism."
- p. 3 (systems image their design groups): "given any system design, someone someday will find a better one to do the same job." -- called an article of faith among experienced designers; speaking of the design for a job is misleading outside its context of space, time, knowledge and technology.
- p. 3 (systems image their design groups): "the quantum jumps in compiler design progress were associated with the appearance of new groups of people" -- first university research teams, then independent software houses.
- p. 3 (systems image their design groups): "To the extent that an organization is not completely flexible in its communication structure, that organization will stamp out an image of itself in every design it produces." -- the larger the organization, the more pronounced.
- p. 3 (examples): "five people were assigned to the COBOL job and three to the ALGOL job." -- result: "The resulting COBOL compiler ran in five phases, the ALGOL compiler ran in three."
- p. 3 (examples): "After great effort they produced a copy of their organization chart." -- two military services directed to develop a common weapon system; and a computer system's hardware, system software and application program correspond to engineers, system programmers and application programmers.
- p. 4 (system management): "The structures of large systems tend to disintegrate during development, qualitatively more so than with small systems."
- p. 4 (system management): "The process seems to occur in three steps, the first two of which are controllable" -- too many people assigned; conventional management breaks the communication structure; the homomorphism makes the system reflect it.
- p. 4 (system management): "to delegate tasks when the apparent complexity of the system approaches his limits of comprehension." -- the turning point: the initial designer either reduces the system to comprehensibility or loses control; the outcome is almost predictable under schedule and budget pressure.
- p. 4 (system management): "A manager knows that he will be vulnerable to the charge of mismanagement if he misses his schedule without having applied all his resources" -- this forces delegation.
- p. 4 (system management): "the unit of resource is the dollar, and all resources must be measured using units of measurement which are convertible to the dollar." -- the conventional accounting reasoning about resources.
- p. 4 (system management): "One fallacy behind this calculation is the property of linearity" -- two men for a year and one hundred men for a week are not equal resources; they cannot work in the same structure and will not design similar systems.
- p. 4 (system management): "Assumptions which may be adequate for peeling potatoes and erecting brick walls fail for designing systems."
- p. 4 (system management): "Probably the greatest single common factor behind many poorly designed systems now in existence has been the availability of a design organization in need of work." -- linked to Parkinson's Law: prestige tied to budget size motivates expansion.
- p. 4 (system management): "the number of possible communication paths in an organization is approximately half the square of the number of people in the organization." -- so communication must be restricted for work to get done.
- p. 4 (system management): "This is one reason why militarystyle organizations design systems which look like their organization charts." -- each individual has at most one superior and about seven subordinates.
- p. 4 (conclusion): "organizations which design systems (in the broad sense used here) are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations." -- the basic thesis.
- p. 4 (conclusion): "a design effort should be organized according to the need for communication." -- the criterion derived.
- p. 4 (conclusion): "Because the design which occurs first is almost never the best possible, the prevailing system concept may need to change." -- hence flexibility of organization is important.
- p. 4 (conclusion): "Ways must be found to reward design managers for keeping their organizations lean and flexible." -- and a philosophy is needed that does not assume that adding manpower simply adds to productivity.

## Readings for our work
- The repository's structure will copy the structure of whoever produces it. When work is split among parallel agents or sessions, each with its own scope, the tools will split along the same lines (one script per agent, duplicated helpers). Check: after a multi-agent session, look for near-duplicate helpers across new files; merge them before commit. Rests on: homomorphism, stamp out an image of itself, delegation narrows alternatives. Would not apply if: the agents share one module and its interface is fixed before delegation.
- Checks written by the same hand that writes the product embody the same communication path, so they will not find what that hand cannot see. A check should come from a separate step with its own view (for example a quotation checker against the source, not the writer's memory). Rests on: no group both organized and unbiased, footnote on groups not compromising their own design. Would not apply if: the check is mechanical and fully specified by an external source (such as the XML records).
- The interface between our repository and the GitLab DLL repository is a negotiated interface specification between two groups; patches should cross it as small, self-describing units (one concern per merge request, original line endings kept) so that the other side can accept them without needing our context. Rests on: interface definition, negotiated interface specification. Would not apply if: the same person owns and reviews both repositories with no other maintainers.
- Organize work by the need for communication: things that must change together (a figure, its registry entry, its template) belong in one place and one commit; things that need not (a check change and an analysis) belong in separate commits. Check: commits that touch both method/ checks and research/ text without a stated link. Rests on: criterion for structuring. Would not apply if: the change to the check is caused by and verified with the analysis.
- The first design is almost never the best, so the tool set must be kept cheap to reorganize; resist growing the method because effort is available. Rests on: availability of a design organization in need of work, first design almost never best, Parkinson's Law. Would not apply if: each tool addition was triggered by a question no existing source answers.
