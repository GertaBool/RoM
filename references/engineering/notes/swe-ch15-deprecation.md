# Hyrum Wright (edited by Tom Manshreck), Deprecation, chapter 15 of Software Engineering at Google (O'Reilly, 2020; the text gives the book title and the CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 licence, publisher, year and chapter number are taken from the assignment)

## What it says
- Introduction: "Old systems require continued maintenance, esoteric expertise, and generally more work as they diverge from the surrounding ecosystem." -- why obsolete systems cost.
- Introduction: "We refer to the process of orderly migration away from and eventual removal of obsolete systems as deprecation." -- the definition.
- Introduction: "poorly deprecated systems may cost more than leaving them alone." -- deprecation done badly is worse than none; it can be planned during design.
- Introduction: "Google is still learning how best to deprecate and remove software systems." -- stated limit of the chapter's authority; it covers technical systems where the owner has visibility into use.
- Why Deprecate?: "our discussion of deprecation begins from the fundamental premise that code is a liability, not an asset." -- the premise; ongoing costs include operations and updating as ecosystems evolve.
- Why Deprecate?: "The age of a system alone doesn’t justify its deprecation." -- LaTeX as the example; "Just because something is old, it does not follow that it is obsolete."
- Why Deprecate?: "Deprecation is best suited for systems that are demonstrably obsolete and a replacement exists that provides comparable functionality." -- the condition for deprecation.
- Why Deprecate?: "having multiple systems performing the same function also impedes the evolution of the newer system because it is still expected to maintain compatibility with the old one." -- cost of two systems; they may come to depend on each other.
- Why Deprecate?: "Code itself doesn’t bring value: it is the functionality that it provides that brings value." -- functionality is the asset, code a means.
- Why Deprecate?: "we should instead focus on how much functionality it can deliver per unit of code and try to maximize that metric." -- removing excess code is one of the easiest ways.
- Why Deprecate?: "organizations have a limit on the amount of deprecation work that is reasonable to undergo simultaneously" -- road-paving analogy; choose projects with care and commit to finishing them.
- Why Is Deprecation So Hard?: "the more users of a system, the higher the probability that users are using it in unexpected and unforeseen ways, and the harder it will be to deprecate and remove such a system." -- Hyrum's Law; usage just happens to work instead of being guaranteed.
- Why Is Deprecation So Hard?: "removing a system can be thought of as the ultimate change: we aren’t just changing behavior, we are removing that behavior completely!" -- removal shakes loose unexpected dependents.
- Why Is Deprecation So Hard?: "a one-to-one match between the old system and the new system is rare, and every use of the old system must be evaluated in the context of the new one." -- the functional difference problem.
- Why Is Deprecation So Hard?: "Another surprising reluctance to deprecate is emotional attachment to old systems, particularly those that the deprecator had a hand in helping to create." -- change aversion; addressed by keeping removed code findable in history.
- Why Is Deprecation So Hard?: "there are two ways of doing things: the one that’s deprecated, and the one that’s not-yet-ready." -- the Google joke; good documentation and signposts help.
- Why Is Deprecation So Hard?: "the costs of doing nothing and letting the system lumber along unattended are not readily observable." -- political difficulty of funding deprecation.
- Why Is Deprecation So Hard?: "Within Google, we’ve observed that migrating to entirely new systems is extremely expensive, and the costs are frequently underestimated." -- hence evolving in situ.
- Why Is Deprecation So Hard?: "Incremental deprecation efforts accomplished by in-place refactoring can keep existing systems running while making it easier to deliver value to users." -- recommendation.
- Deprecation During Design: "its eventual decommissioning after a lifetime of productive service must be taken into account, even going so far as to allocate funds for this purpose." -- nuclear power plant analogy; footnote 1 cites IAEA Technical Reports Series No. 382 (1997).
- Deprecation During Design: "How easy will it be for my consumers to migrate from my product to a potential replacement?" -- design question; the other is "How can I replace parts of my system incrementally?"
- Deprecation During Design: "After a software system exists, the only remaining options are support it, carefully deprecate it, or let it stop functioning when some external event causes it to break." -- the three options.
- Deprecation During Design: "don’t start projects that your organization isn’t committed to support for the expected lifespan of the organization." -- recommendation.
- Types of Deprecation: "Deprecation isn’t a single kind of process, but a continuum of them" -- divided into advisory and compulsory.
- Advisory Deprecation: "Advisory deprecations are those that don’t have a deadline and aren’t high priority for the organization" -- definition; "These could also be labeled aspirational deprecations"; "This kind of deprecation often lacks enforcement".
- Advisory Deprecation: "Such a new system should not be considered in a beta period: it should be ready for production uses and loads and should be prepared to support new users indefinitely." -- condition on the replacement.
- Advisory Deprecation: "the benefits cannot be simply incremental: they must be transformative." -- when advisory deprecation works.
- Advisory Deprecation: "this can lead to (slightly) fewer new uses of an obsolete system, but it rarely leads to teams actively migrating away from it." -- effect of a deprecation warning alone.
- Advisory Deprecation: "comparatively many uses of the old system will tend to pick up a large share of new uses" -- the pull of existing uses.
- Compulsory Deprecation: "This kind of deprecation usually comes with a deadline for removal of the obsolete system" -- definition.
- Compulsory Deprecation: "the best way for compulsory deprecation efforts to scale is by localizing the expertise of migrating users to within a single team of experts" -- usually the team removing the old system; LSC tools apply.
- Compulsory Deprecation: "For compulsory deprecation to actually work, its schedule needs to have an enforcement mechanism." -- power to break noncompliant users after sufficient warning.
- Compulsory Deprecation: "compulsory deprecations without staffing to do the work can come across to customer teams as mean spirited" -- unfunded mandate; the authors "strongly advocate that compulsory deprecations are actively staffed by a specialized team through completion."
- Compulsory Deprecation: "It is hard to believe the deprecation is really compulsory if that team can veto its progress." -- political limit when the last user is critical.
- Compulsory Deprecation: "These intentional changes provide a mechanism for discovering unintended dependencies by seeing what breaks" -- turning the old system off temporarily; renaming implementation-only symbols to find unaware users.
- Compulsory Deprecation: "the team will announce planned outages of increasing duration in the months and weeks prior to the turndown." -- like DiRT exercises; for static dependencies static analysis is often sufficient.
- Deprecation Warnings: "Deprecation warnings can help prevent new uses, but rarely lead to migration of existing systems." -- and "hope is not a strategy".
- Deprecation Warnings: "these warnings can soon overwhelm users of a system to the point where they ignore them altogether." -- alert fatigue from transitive warnings.
- Deprecation Warnings: "Any deprecation warning issued to a user needs to have two properties: actionability and relevance." -- the two properties.
- Deprecation Warnings: "A warning is actionable if the user can use the warning to actually perform some relevant action, not just in theory, but in practical terms" -- definition of actionable.
- Deprecation Warnings: "A warning is relevant if it surfaces at a time when a user actually performs the indicated action." -- definition of relevant; best while writing the code, not weeks after check-in.
- Deprecation Warnings: "It’s important to resist the urge to put deprecation warnings on everything possible." -- warnings are limited to newly changed lines; intrusive warnings only for compulsory deprecations.
- Process Owners: "without explicit owners, a deprecation process is unlikely to make meaningful progress, no matter how many warnings and alerts a system might generate." -- ownership finding.
- Process Owners: "The second case becomes simply an advisory deprecation, which will never organically finish, and the first is a commitment to maintain every old system ad infinitum." -- the alternatives to owners.
- Process Owners: "These teams should have removal as their primary goal, not just a side project of some other work." -- otherwise deprecation is always lower priority; abandoned projects do not fade on their own.
- Milestones: "it can often feel that the only milestone of a deprecation process is removing the obsolete system entirely." -- the problem; that step is the least noticed outside the team.
- Milestones: "managing a team working on deprecation should involve concrete incremental milestones, which are measurable and deliver value to users." -- such as deleting a key subcomponent.
- Deprecation Tooling: "These tools can be categorized as discovery, migration, and backsliding prevention tooling." -- the three kinds.
- Discovery: "Much of the initial work of deprecation is determining who is using the old system—and in which unanticipated ways." -- may require revisiting the decision.
- Discovery: "we treat our global test suite as an oracle to determine whether all references to an old symbol have been removed." -- customers are responsible for sufficient testing.
- Migration: "The LSC process and tooling are particularly useful in managing the large effort of actually updating the codebase to refer to new libraries or runtime services." -- migration tooling.
- Preventing backsliding: "an often overlooked piece of deprecation infrastructure is tooling for preventing the addition of new uses of the very thing being actively removed." -- backsliding prevention.
- Preventing backsliding: "Without backsliding prevention, deprecation can become a game of whack-a-mole" -- users add new uses from familiarity or examples; "both counterproductive and demoralizing".
- Preventing backsliding: "Tricorder surfaces new uses of these symbols at review time." -- micro level, via compiler annotations; at macro level, build visibility whitelists that tooling prunes as users migrate.
- Conclusion: "A complete deprecation process involves successfully managing social and technical challenges through policy and tooling." -- summary.
- TL;DRs: "Removing things is often more difficult than building them to begin with because existing users are often using the system beyond its original design." -- also "Evolving a system in place is usually cheaper than replacing it with a new one, when turndown costs are included."
- TL;DRs: "These ecosystem costs are diffuse and difficult to measure. Deprecation and removal costs are often similarly diffuse." -- limit on cost evaluation.

## Readings for our work
- Reading (inference): our tools and checks are code, hence liabilities; each should be judged by the function it delivers per unit of code, and a tool that duplicates another or that no current task uses should be removed, not kept alongside its replacement. Rests on: code is a liability; functionality per unit of code; two systems impede evolution of the newer. Would not apply if: the old tool is still the only producer of some output a current document cites.
- Reading (inference): when a tool or check is superseded, a deprecation note alone will not move uses; the replacement must be wired in everywhere (the gate, the renderer, the docs) by the person deprecating, with a date and a removal step. Rests on: advisory deprecation rarely leads to migration; compulsory needs enforcement and staffing; explicit owners. Would not apply if: the old tool has no callers, in which case delete it at once.
- Reading (inference): prevent backsliding mechanically: once a pattern is retired (for example, a hand-edited generated file, or typed numbers in a document), the pre-commit gate should flag new instances in changed lines only, with the replacement named in the message. Rests on: Preventing backsliding (Tricorder at review time); warnings limited to newly changed lines; actionable and relevant warnings. Would not apply if: the pattern cannot be detected without false positives large enough to cause alert fatigue.
- Reading (inference): checks that print many warnings get ignored; our check runner should fail on a small set of actionable findings and keep the rest silent, rather than accumulate advisory output. Rests on: alert fatigue; resist putting warnings on everything; actionability and relevance. Would not apply if: the warnings are read and acted on in practice (measurable from commit history).
- Reading (inference): prefer evolving the legacy DLL in place through small patches over replacing subsystems wholesale; replacement costs are usually underestimated. Rests on: migrating to entirely new systems is extremely expensive; incremental in-place refactoring; TL;DR on evolving in place. Would not apply if: the subsystem cannot be tested in place and a replacement can be validated by the harness against the old one.
- Reading (inference): to find hidden dependencies before removing something from the DLL or our tools, turn it off temporarily (rename an internal symbol, disable a script) and run the build and the checks to see what breaks. Rests on: intentional changes discover unintended dependencies; renaming implementation-only symbols. Would not apply if: static analysis already lists all users, as the chapter says is often the case for static code.
- Reading (inference): do not start a tool or document line that we will not maintain for the life of the project; the decision to support is made at creation. Rests on: Deprecation During Design (only options after existence; don't start what you won't support). Would not apply if: the tool is explicitly one-off and is deleted after use.
- Reading (inference): deprecation progress should be measured by incremental milestones (for example, the UNCHECKED list shrinking by named files), not only by final removal. Rests on: Milestones (concrete incremental milestones). Would not apply if: the removal is a single step.
