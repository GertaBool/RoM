## What it says
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "You come up with an itch. You code it up. You do not need any pre-authorization from the project to do so." -- the cycle starts with the contributor.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "the reviews will be done to assess the merit of various things, like the general idea behind your patch" -- review covers whether the problem is worth solving, the design, and the implementation.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "Your goal is *not* necessarily to convince others that what you are building is good." -- the goal is to get help towards a better solution than one can build alone.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "The people who may need to know are the ones who worked on the code you are touching." -- they have no obligation to help; git log -p finds them.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "It is also a good idea to check whether your topic has been discussed previously on the mailing list, or whether similar work is already in progress." -- to avoid duplicated work.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "Tell reviewers which suggestions you plan to use, which ones you disagree with, and when a comment leads you to consider a different approach." -- respond in the discussion, not only with a new series.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "Defend your design decisions on the list first and work with reviewers and other members to improve the design before revising the implementation." -- this avoids wasted effort on an implementation before the design is solid.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "Topics with unresolved fundamental design critiques will not be considered ready for merging." -- design objections block merging.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "As a rough default, avoid sending more than one new version of the same series per day" -- waiting collects broader input and avoids churn.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "These early update iterations are expected to be full replacements, not incremental updates on top of what you posted already." -- mistakes are fixed by rewriting history.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "Just present a logical progression made by a perfect developer who makes no mistakes while working on the topic." -- the series shows the result, not the path.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "Being in `seen` has no other meaning." -- queuing in seen does not mean the patch was accepted in any way.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "by the time a topic gets merged to 'next', it is expected that everybody agrees that the scope and the basic direction of the topic are appropriate" -- later updates are small corrections; after about 7 calendar days in next without tweaks a topic goes to master.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "If a mailing list discussion convinces you that your changes aren't ideal, please explicitly retract the topic to save the maintainer time and effort." -- and notify the list when dropping a topic.
- A typical life cycle of a patch series: "Topics with unaddressed review comments that remain inactive for four weeks may be discarded by the maintainer." -- a stated time limit.
- Choose a starting point: "always base your work on the oldest integration branch that your change is relevant to" -- maint for bug fixes in the released version, master otherwise.
- Choose a starting point: "This also means that `next` or `seen` are inappropriate starting points for your work" -- they only exist to check that topics in flight work together and are force-pushed.
- Choose a starting point: "A topic that is literally built on top of `next` cannot be merged to `master` without dragging in all the other topics in `next`, some of which may not be ready." -- the cost of a wrong base.
- Choose a starting point: "you may want to create your own custom base-branch by forking `master` and merging the required topic branches into it." -- only under truly exceptional circumstances, and the cover letter must say how it was made.
- Make separate commits: "you should not be sending out a patch that was generated between your working tree and your commit head." -- always commit with a full message and generate patches from the repository.
- Make separate commits: "Give an explanation for the change(s) that is detailed enough so that people can judge if it is good thing to do, without reading the actual patch text" -- the message must stand on its own.
- Make separate commits: "If your description starts to get too long, that's a sign that you probably need to split up your commit to finer grained pieces." -- message length as a signal for splitting.
- Make separate commits: "Descriptions that summarize the point in the subject well, and describe the motivation for the change, the approach taken by the change" -- and how it differs from the prior version.
- Make separate commits: "Make sure that you have tests for the bug you are fixing." -- see t/README.
- Make separate commits: "make sure that you have new tests to show the feature triggers the new behavior when it should, and to show the feature does not trigger when it shouldn't." -- tests for both directions.
- Make separate commits: "After any code change, make sure that the entire test suite passes." -- the whole suite, every time.
- Make separate commits: "When fixing a bug, make sure you have new tests that break if somebody else breaks what you fixed by accident to avoid regression." -- regression tests.
- Make separate commits: "try merging your work to 'next' and 'seen' and make sure the tests still pass" -- topics in flight may interact.
- Make separate commits: "Do not forget to update the documentation to describe the updated behavior" -- and check that it formats well.
- Make separate commits: "A huge patch that touches the files all over the place only to correct the inconsistency is not welcome, though." -- spelling consistency is fixed gradually, alongside other real work; potential clashes "are not worth it."
- Make separate commits: "preferably submitted as independent patches separate from other documentation changes." -- obvious typo fixes go in their own patches.
- Make separate commits: "We are picky about whitespaces." -- changes must not trigger errors with the sample pre-commit hook; run git diff --check before committing.
- Describe your changes well: "The log message that explains your changes is just as important as the changes themselves." -- future developers need to know why.
- Describe your changes well: "you often discover your own bugs yourself, while writing the log message to summarize the thought behind it" -- writing the message is also a check.
- Describe your changes well: "Writing down why you excluded what the code does not do will help guide future developers." -- record what is left out and why.
- Describe your changes well: "The goal of your log message is to convey the _why_ behind your change to help future developers." -- reviewers check the message serves this.
- Describe your changes well: "The first line of the commit message should be a short description (50 characters is the soft limit" -- no full stop, conventionally prefixed with an area.
- Describe your changes well: "explains the problem the change tries to solve, i.e. what is wrong with the current code without the change." -- first element of a meaningful body.
- Describe your changes well: "justifies the way the change solves the problem, i.e. why the result with the change is better." -- second element; then alternatives considered and discarded.
- Describe your changes well: "records the resolution of design or viability concerns raised by the community during the review" -- so the record explains why the approach was chosen.
- Describe your changes well: "The problem statement that describes the status quo is written in the present tense." -- the status quo is the code without the change.
- Describe your changes well: "Describe your changes in imperative mood" -- as if giving orders to the codebase.
- Describe your changes well: "Try to make sure your explanation can be understood without external resources." -- summarise a discussion instead of giving a URL.
- Describe your changes well: "A commit that introduced the root cause of a bug you are fixing." -- one reason to reference another commit, in the format abbreviated hash (subject, date).
- Certify your work: "To improve tracking of who did what, we ask you to certify that you wrote the patch or have the right to pass it on under the same license as ours" -- the sign-off; without it patches are not accepted.
- Certify your work: "a record of the contribution (including all personal information I submit with it, including my sign-off) is maintained indefinitely" -- part (d) of the Developer's Certificate of Origin 1.1.
- Certify your work: "Please use a known identity in the `Signed-off-by:` trailer, since we cannot accept anonymous contributions." -- a pseudonym is acceptable if distinctive, identifying and not misleading.
- Commit trailers: "`Reviewed-by:`, unlike the other trailers, can only be offered by the reviewers themselves when they are completely satisfied with the patch after a detailed analysis." -- one of the common trailers (Reported-by, Acked-by, Tested-by, Co-authored-by, Helped-by, Suggested-by and others).
- Commit trailers: "`Tested-by:` is used to indicate that the person applied the patch and found it to have the desired effect." -- a testing trailer.
- Commit trailers: "trailers are added in chronological order" -- so it is clear which person added which trailer.
- Cover Letter: "Every code change comes with risk of regression and maintenance cost." -- the cover letter should say why the change is worth that.
- Cover Letter: "Make sure your target audience can understand what the patches are about and why they are needed without prior context." -- and give a summary of changes since previous rounds.
- Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI): "It's not yet clear that this can be legally satisfied when submitting significant amount of content that has been generated by AI tools." -- the Certificate of Origin problem.
- Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI): "AIs still often hallucinate or just produce bad code, commit messages, documentation or output, even when you point out their mistakes." -- the second stated issue.
- Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI): "we will reject anything that looks AI generated, that sounds overly formal or bloated, that looks like AI slop, that looks good on the surface but makes no sense" -- or that senders cannot explain.
- Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI): "They can also use AI to help with debugging, or with checking for obvious mistakes" -- the recommended use: step-by-step help rather than a full solution to copy.
- Generate your patch using Git tools: "Git based diff tools generate unidiff which is the preferred format." -- -M for renames is fine.
- Generate your patch using Git tools: "Please make sure your patch does not add commented out debugging code, or include any extra files which do not relate to what your patch is trying to achieve." -- review the generated patch.
- Generate your patch using Git tools: "please make sure it cleanly applies to the starting point you have chosen" -- master is the default expected starting point; otherwise say so in the cover letter.
- Sending your patches: "Patches that may be security relevant should be submitted privately to the Git Security mailing list" -- not the public list.
- Sending your patches: "These commands are optimized for the workflow of sending patches, avoiding many ways your existing e-mail client ... might render your patches unusable." -- format-patch and send-email; b4 is encouraged for bookkeeping.
- Sending your patches: "It is important for a developer to be able to" quote "your changes, using standard e-mail tools" -- hence each patch goes inline in a separate message.
- Sending your patches: "Be wary of your MUAs word-wrap corrupting your patch. Do not cut-n-paste your patch; you can lose tabs that way if you are not careful." -- a stated warning.
- Sending your patches: "You often want to add additional explanation about the patch, other than the commit message itself." -- such cover-letter material goes between the three-dash line and the diffstat.
- Sending your patches: "If your patch series is part of a larger effort spanning multiple patch series, briefly describe the broader goal, and state where the current series fits into that goal." -- placing each series in the whole.
- Sending your patches: "Do not let your e-mail client send format=flowed which would destroy whitespaces in your patches." -- no MIME attachments or quoted-printable.
- Sending your patches: "Your patch is not judged by who you are" -- a good patch from an unknown origin beats a poor one from a respected origin; do not PGP sign.
- Handling Conflicts and Iterating Patches: "you have textual or semantic conflicts coming from the difference between the old base and the base you used to build" -- when a series applies but does not build or test, or does not apply.
- Handling Conflicts and Iterating Patches: "This trial merge may conflict. It is primarily to see what conflicts _other_ topics may have with your topic." -- note seen conflicts in the cover letter.
- Subsystems with dedicated maintainers: "Patches to these parts should be based on their trees." -- some parts live in separate repositories.
- GitHub CI: "If a branch does not pass all test cases then it will be marked with a red +x+, instead of a green check." -- CI runs on every push to a fork.
- GitHub CI: "retract your breaking topic quickly while you fix the breakage you caused." -- watch the maintainer's pushes for new failures.
- MUA specific hints: "Please make sure your MUA is set up properly not to corrupt whitespaces." -- test by mailing to oneself and applying with git am.
- MUA specific hints: "If what is in the resulting commit is not exactly what you would want to see, it is very likely that your maintainer would end up hand editing the log message" -- check the result of a trial application.
- MUA specific hints: "It tends to screw up non-ASCII characters (most notably in people's names), and also whitespaces (fatal in patches)." -- the Gnus case of MIME-decoded piping.

## Readings for our work
- Inference: a commit message (and a merge request description to the GitLab DLL repository) should state the problem in the present tense, why the change solves it, the alternatives discarded, and what is deliberately left out, readable without the session transcript or a URL. Check: each message has those parts; a reader can judge the change without the diff. Rests on: Describe your changes well bullets; Make separate commits explanation bullet. Would not apply if: the change is trivial (the source itself exempts really trivial patches from the full discipline).
- Inference: a commit message that grows long is a signal to split the commit; use it as a review prompt against mixed-concern commits. Rests on: the description-too-long bullet. Would not apply if: the length comes from evidence (figures, test output) rather than from several changes.
- Inference: the DLL patches must be whitespace-clean against the target repository (CRLF files): run git diff --check, and check that the diff touches only intended lines (no full-file line-ending rewrite) before delivery. Rests on: We are picky about whitespaces; review the generated patch for unrelated content; MUA hints on corrupted whitespace. Would not apply if: the upstream repository normalises line endings itself on apply (it does not by default).
- Inference: generate DLL patches from commits (format-patch, or a pushed branch), never from the working tree or by copy and paste, and apply each to the chosen base in a clean clone before sending. Rests on: Make separate commits (no working-tree patch); clean apply to the starting point; do not cut-n-paste. Would not apply if: the upstream builds patches itself from a branch.
- Inference: each fix gets a test that fails without it, and each new behaviour a test for both triggering and not triggering; the whole check suite runs after any change. In our repository this means a check or experiment that would break if the fix were reverted. Rests on: tests bullets in Make separate commits. Would not apply if: the effect follows from a formula and is computed (our CLAUDE.md rule 9), where the test is the computation.
- Inference: for review, the trailer Reviewed-by may only be given by the reviewer; analogously, a check or approval written by the same hand that it checks is not a review. A gate's approval should come from a different person or a mechanism the author cannot edit in the same commit. Rests on: Commit trailers Reviewed-by bullet. Would not apply if: the project has only one contributor and no other reviewer exists; then say so instead of claiming review.
- Inference: AI-written content must be explainable by its sender and free of bloat and of claims that look good but make no sense; for our work, every claim in a document or message must be traceable to a source and every change explainable, which matches the rule against claims from memory. Rests on: Use of Artificial Intelligence bullets. Would not apply if: the content is mechanical output of a tool (e.g. a generated table) whose generator is itself reviewed.
- Inference: an iteration of a merge request should be a full replacement presenting a clean logical progression, and design objections should be resolved before more implementation work. Rests on: life cycle bullets on full replacements, perfect developer, design first. Would not apply if: the series is already merged to a shared branch (then incremental fixes on top, as for next).
- Inference: when a DLL change depends on another unmerged change, state the base and how it was built in the merge request, and do not build on an integration branch. Rests on: Choose a starting point bullets. Would not apply if: the upstream has a single branch.
