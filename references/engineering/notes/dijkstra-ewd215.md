# Edsger W. Dijkstra, A Case against the GO TO Statement (EWD215; published as Go To Statement Considered Harmful, Comm. ACM 11, March 1968)
Source: lit3/dijkstra-ewd215/doc.txt. Read: lines 1-125 (all).

## What it says
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, opening: "the quality of programmers is a decreasing function of the density of go to statements in the programs they produce." -- presented as a long-standing observation.
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, claim: "the go to statement should be abolished from all" higher level languages, with the exception "everything except —perhaps— plain machine code".
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, first remark: "the process taking place under control of his program is the true subject matter of his activity" -- the program is made by the programmer, but the process it generates is what must meet the specification.
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, delegation: "once the program has been made, the" making of the process "is delegated to the machine."
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, second remark: "our intellectual powers are rather geared to master static relations and that our powers to visualize processes evolving in time are relatively poorly developed."
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, recommendation: "shorten the conceptual gap between the static program and the dynamic process" -- to make the correspondence between program in text space and process in time "as trivial as possible".
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, characterizing progress: "what data do we have to fix in order that we can redo the process until that very same point?" -- the test question for describing where a process is.
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, textual index: "Let us call such a pointer to a suitable place in the text" a textual index. -- for a pure concatenation of assignments, one point in the text suffices.
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, conditionals: "the fact remains that the progress of the process remains characterized by a single textual index." -- holds for if, if-else, case clauses and conditional expressions.
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, procedures: "we can characterize the progress of the process via a sequence of textual indices, the length of this sequence being equal to the dynamic depth of procedure calling."
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, repetition: "makes us well equipped to retain our intellectual grasp on the processes generated by repetition clauses." -- the reasoning pattern meant is induction. -- repetition clauses are kept for realism although recursion could replace them.
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, dynamic index: "inexorably counting the ordinal number of the corresponding current repetition." -- this is what the author calls a dynamic index, associated with each entry into a repetition clause.
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, general result: "the progress of the process can always be uniquely characterized by a (mixed) sequence of textual and/or dynamic indices."
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, main point: "the values of these indices are outside programmer's control" -- "They provide independent coordinates in which to describe the progress of the process."
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, why coordinates are needed: "we can interpret the value of a variable only with respect to the progress of the process." -- illustrated by counting people entering a room, where n is briefly the count minus one.
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, effect of go to: "it becomes terribly hard to find a meaningful set of coordinates in which to describe the process progress."
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, variables cannot serve as coordinates: "it is relative to the progress that the meaning of these values is to be understood!"
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, action counter: "such a coordinate, although unique, is utterly unhelpful" -- a count of actions since program start describes progress uniquely but makes it very complicated to locate points of interest.
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, verdict: "The go to statement as it stands is just too primitive, it is too much an invitation to make a mess of one's program."
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, stated limit: "I do not claim that the clauses mentioned are exhaustive in the sense that they will satisfy all needs" -- any further clauses (e.g. abortion clauses) must keep "a programmer independent coordinate system" maintainable.
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, acknowledgement: "Heinz Zemanek at the pre-ALGOL meeting in early 1959 in Copenhagen quite explicitly expressed his doubts" -- about treating go to on equal footing with assignment.
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, prior remarks: "restrict the use of the go to statement to alarm exits" -- a recommendation the author could not trace, presumed to be Hoare's; Wirth and Hoare are quoted that the case construction "mirrors the dynamic structure of a program more clearly than go to statements and switches".
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, Jacopini: "The exercise to translate an arbitrary flow diagram more or less mechanically into a jumpless one, however, is not to be recommended." -- the result "cannot be expected to be more transparent than the original one."
- A Case against the GO TO Statement, transcription note: "The additions are absent from the typescript furnished to the archive but are present in the version published in Comm. ACM 11" -- the last two paragraphs and references come from the published version.

## Readings for our work
- Analysis scripts over game logs should be written so that a reader can say from the text where the process is and what each variable means at that point: straight pipelines of named steps (load, filter, aggregate, render), not control flow driven by flags and early jumps. Check: each tool in method/ and research/ can be described as a sequence of steps; flag a tool whose behaviour depends on global mutable state. Rests on: second remark, recommendation, why coordinates are needed. Would not apply if: the tool is a small one-off whose output is checked directly.
- A replay of a seeded experiment needs fixed coordinates of progress (turn, player, seed, step) that do not depend on the programmer's choices; figures should be keyed by such coordinates, as the turn rule already requires. Rests on: characterizing progress, main point, why coordinates are needed. Would not apply if: the harness already records seed and turn for every logged event.
- In the legacy C++ DLL, patches should not add new goto-like exits or flag-driven jumps; where the existing code has them, a mechanical rewrite to remove them is not recommended by the author and would also bloat the patch. Keep patches minimal and structured. Rests on: verdict, Jacopini. Would not apply if: a rewrite is needed to fix a defect and is reviewed as its own change.
- A rule (or check) that only counts a property, like the action counter, can be unique and still unhelpful; checks should be judged by whether they help locate the points that matter, not only by whether they are precise. Rests on: action counter. Would not apply if: the count is itself the quantity of interest.
